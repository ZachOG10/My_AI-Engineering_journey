# Summary of Andrew Ng's Letters on AI Engineering core skills

## Part 1: The Big Picture

In the first letter on AI engineering, Andrew Ng acknowledges that AI has made working in the AI and software engineering space more effective and easier today than in the years before 2022. He states how this shift has helped people who have the skill to take advantage of it by building better and amazing projects, and how this skill gives them better access to job opportunities.

However, he states that the space is noisy because people lack focus on building the skills that really matter to be truly valuable engineers. He then points out the essential skills engineers need to learn, and what skills employers need to look for in developers or engineers.

### The skills he says are important

- Building and deploying AI applications
- Software engineering fundamentals
- Using coding agents
- Shaping the build

### Building and deploying AI applications

He points out how important it is for developers to know how to work with clouds, stating that a good number of developers lack actual skills in this area. He cites that a developer must have good knowledge about LLMs, context engineering, RAG, agentic workflows, machine learning, and deep learning to understand, build, and deploy AI applications that have more predictable behaviors.

### Software engineering fundamentals

He states the importance of having a deep understanding of how software really works, as it gives engineers better knowledge of how to build highly effective software and helps developers recognize what trade-offs exist. This leads to better decisions in choosing a software stack, designing system architecture, designing data stores, and testing. Having this full knowledge keeps such developers ahead of engineers who just use coding agents to vibe code.

### Using coding agents

Having this deep knowledge of how software works and how to build it will help developers manage a coding agent's context and use agents more effectively and appropriately. Developers need to evolve as tools change to strengthen their workflow.

### Shaping the build

AI engineers should be able to decide what specifications a piece of software should have and what it should do, without being given an already made plan or architecture. This means engineers should have great product sense and understand the business context and customer goals, so they know exactly what to build and how it aligns with customer expectations. All of these skills give you the ability to recognize opportunities and take proper and responsible actions.

## Part 2: Building and Deploying AI Applications

In the second letter, Andrew Ng breaks down the first main skill from his map: building and deploying AI applications. He points out six main things you need to know here:

1. LLM foundations
2. Grounding models with data
3. Building agentic systems
4. Evaluation-driven development
5. Operating in production
6. Machine learning foundations

He explains that regular software is predictable but rigid, while AI software is flexible but unpredictable. Because AI can make mistakes, good AI engineering is all about building safety nets around it using checks, fallbacks, and plain code for things that have to be exact. He also notes that while regular software engineering needs heavy planning up front, AI engineering works better with quick, small experiments where you notice where the AI fails and fix it fast.

### The first three sub-skills

- **LLM foundations:** LLMs read and write text one token at a time. Knowing this simple fact helps you balance cost, speed, and quality, and helps you decide when to switch models, fine-tune, or host a model yourself.
- **Grounding models with data:** To make LLMs truly useful, you need to ground them with good data. This goes beyond basic RAG to choosing what goes into the prompt, cleaning messy files, and keeping information fresh.
- **Building agentic systems:** His advice is to keep it simple. Use regular code where you need precise answers, use AI where you need understanding, and always set clear limits so the AI doesn't break things or leak private data.

## Part 3: Software Engineering Fundamentals

In the third letter he explains that even when an AI agent writes all your code, you still need to understand software fundamentals. Without them, you can't tell the agent what you really want or how to put it to the agent, and you won't even know what choices are available to be made.

This letter describes what the study of AI engineering skills shows are the most important things to know in software engineering. Even if an AI agent writes your code, you still need to know how software really works, or you can't guide it toward the right choices or judge what it builds.

### The five areas that matter

1. Building full-stack applications
2. Managing data
3. Designing how the pieces fit together
4. Making things secure and reliable
5. Running it in production

### Key points

- Data deserves extra care because the AI only knows what your data gives it, so a badly chosen setup means it doesn't know what it's missing.
- Memorizing syntax is becoming less useful, but understanding how software works is not.
- The people who understand the basics beat the ones who just vibe code, because they know what's possible and what choices are available.

## Part 4: Using Coding Agents

In the fourth letter, he states that using coding agents is a key skill for AI engineers. The better you are at guiding them, the more you get done, and not only with writing code. They can also help with things like analyzing data or running and managing systems.

This skill also changes faster than almost any other, because tools like Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode, and Pi keep getting better, both in how they're built and in the AI models behind them. So you can't learn it once and be done. You have to keep trying new things, building, and learning as the tools change.

### The three-step workflow

Building software with a coding agent follows three steps:

1. **Plan:** Figure out what to build, write down what it should do, and make a step-by-step plan.
2. **Build and check:** The agent builds it, and you check the result with tests or your own eyes.
3. **Release and watch:** You release it and use the agent to watch for problems and fix them.

It's the same basic process as before AI, but now you spend less time typing code and more time deciding what to build, how it should be designed, and whether the result is right. You also go back and repeat steps when something fails.

### The five skills you need

1. Knowing when to spend your effort and when to let the agent work
2. Deciding how much freedom to give the agent
3. Checking its work carefully, since it can make mistakes
4. Setting up the agent with the right notes and tools so it knows your project
5. Understanding roughly how agents work so you can tell when one is going off track

Social media makes it sound like you can just let agents run for hours, but real success usually comes from many small rounds where you step in and use good judgment.

## Part 5: Shaping the Build

In the fifth letter, he said that if you're good at AI engineering, your best work is not just building what someone else planned. You help decide what gets built.

It used to be that product managers and designers decided what to make, and developers just built it, with maybe a project manager keeping track of the schedule. Now those jobs are mixing together. A developer with AI skills can build the software and also help with the planning and design side. And it goes the other way too: product managers and designers are picking up AI engineering skills and starting to build things themselves.

### Moving fast

AI makes building software so fast that if you know how to decide what gets built, you can move quickly without waiting for someone else to tell you what to do. Most software is made by writing a bit of code, getting feedback, and deciding what to do next, over and over. You keep that cycle going fast: make a rough first version to test an idea, then a better version for real users, then add features a little at a time so you're always moving, and pick each next step based on what users say, the risks, and your budget.

### Making choices nobody planned for

If there's no plan, you write one. That means understanding what people really need, having a feel for what looks and works well, and knowing a bit about money, like what things cost and whether it makes a profit. You'll also work with people outside tech, like the marketing team, the money people, and the people who handle laws and rules. Many of them are still figuring out AI, so you get to explain what's possible and help lead the team.

### Taking charge

Many people, even bosses, don't yet know what AI can do, so you can spot problems, suggest fixes, and make them happen without waiting to be told. You own the work, take responsibility when things go wrong, keep going when it gets hard, and keep learning new tools.

This is what makes the work so exciting: you're not just building what you're handed, you're helping decide what gets built, with more power, more freedom, and more impact.
